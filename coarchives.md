#### A gathering on building and reclaiming community knowledge collections

**Concept Note**

[Indigenous People and Local Communities (IPLCs)](https://www.informea.org/en/knowledge/glossary/indigenous-peoples-and-local-communities) is used to refer to people who self-identify as indigenous and belong to a specific local community. Specifically people who have long standing social and cultural relationships with their lands, forests, waters, and ecosystems. Though the use and application of the term “indigenous” by the Government of India is still contested, the term has been increasingly equated to the socially accepted term ‘Adivasi’. In the case of India, the administrative umbrella of “scheduled tribe”  has been used in understanding the state policy towards Adivasis. According to the 2011 Census, over 104 million people, about 8.6% of the population, belong to Scheduled Tribes. However, the status, recognition, and representation of Nomadic and Denotified Tribes (DNTs), along with other unrecognised groups that fall within the broader understanding of IPLCs, remain largely uncharted territory in India, both in policy and in practice. 

Across India, Indigenous/Adivasi peoples and local communities hold an exceptionally diverse range of knowledge systems, developed and transmitted across generations through their relationships with land, biodiversity, livelihoods, culture and community life. These communities hold rich histories, languages, ecological knowledge, songs, stories, and ways of relating to land and life and their living knowledge systems are closely tied to identity and memory. 

Today, there is growing interest in this knowledge from governments, researchers, institutions and individuals. Yet, communities have often been treated as subjects of research or recipients of welfare and upliftment, rather than as custodians of knowledge and as decision makers. In some cases community knowledge has been recorded and circulated without full awareness, consent, or participation. In many cases, communities are not even aware that records, photographs, or data about them exist in archives and collections. While such material can sometimes support *current efforts to strengthen community histories, cultural practices, environmental knowledge, and claims to rights and recognition*; It can also misrepresent communities, remove knowledge from its original context, or provide access to culturally sensitive knowledge without community’s prior consent. In recent years, community groups, collectives, and practitioners across India have started asking important questions:

* Who holds our histories and knowledge?
* Who decides how they are used or shared?
* How can we preserve them for future generations, on our own terms?

Through these conversations, archives and knowledge collections are being reimagined as spaces of self-representation and self-determination. Moving beyond preservation, communities are talking about ensuring a meaningful role in deciding how their knowledge is documented, cared for, and shared. It is about protecting what is sensitive, strengthening intergenerational learning, and supporting communities to build and manage their own archives. This becomes especially important in a time when digital technologies and AI are rapidly expanding the use of data. Community knowledge is increasingly accessed and reused in ways that are not always visible or accountable. Reclaiming archives is also about shaping how knowledge moves into the future, with dignity, consent, and collective control.
